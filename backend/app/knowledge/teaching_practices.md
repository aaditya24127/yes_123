# Teaching best practices: reference for the CRP Mentor Copilot AI

Compiled October 2026 for evaluating classroom lessons in Indian government and low-fee private schools (Classes 1-10).
Everything below is a summary in our own words of the sources listed in section 9; source codes like [S3] point there.
The AI must read this before it judges a lesson, writes student questions, or suggests what a teacher should do next.

---

## 1. Ground rules for the AI

1. **Judge the teaching, not the teacher.** Describe what happened in the lesson and its effect on students. Never label a teacher as "bad" or "weak".
2. **Evidence first.** Every strength or concern must point to something observed: a line in the lesson transcript (with its time), a CRP note, a student answer, or a record (test result, earlier visit). If there is no evidence, say "not observed" rather than guessing.
3. **One lesson is a small sample.** Say how confident you are. A single recording cannot show everything (for example group work that happened before recording started).
4. **Few priorities.** Coaching works best with one or two concrete next steps, not a long list [S12]. Pick the change that would help students most.
5. **Fit the context.** Large classes, multi-grade rooms, few materials, a home language that differs from the textbook language and a pressure to "finish the syllabus" are normal here. Suggest practices that work with chalk, a blackboard, local objects and no electricity.
6. **Compare with the past.** If earlier visits raised an issue, say clearly whether this lesson shows it improved, stayed the same, or got worse.
7. **Cite the research briefly** (for example "checking understanding before moving on [S1, S3]") so the CRP can explain why a practice matters.
8. **Safety.** If the transcript or notes show corporal punishment, humiliation, or discrimination (by gender, caste, religion, disability), flag it as the top concern. These are prohibited under the RTE Act 2009 and are never "a style".

## 2. What good teaching looks like: the observation framework

Adapted from the World Bank **Teach Primary** observation tool [S2] (three areas, nine elements), with Indian curriculum expectations from NCF-SE 2023 [S9] and NIPUN Bharat [S8].
Rate each element 1-5 (1 = rarely seen, 3 = sometimes / partly, 5 = consistently and well). Use "not observed" when the recording cannot show it.

### Area A. Classroom culture
| Element | What you would see or hear at a high level |
|---|---|
| A1 Supportive learning environment | Teacher uses students' names, speaks respectfully, welcomes mistakes as part of learning, treats girls and boys and all social groups equally, notices students who are struggling. |
| A2 Positive behavioural expectations | Clear routines; teacher praises good behaviour specifically; redirects misbehaviour calmly and quickly; little time lost. |
| Time on task | Most of the period is spent on learning (not waiting, copying the board for long stretches, or administrative work). |

### Area B. Instruction
| Element | What you would see or hear at a high level |
|---|---|
| B1 Lesson facilitation | Says what students will learn and why; connects to what they already know; explains in small steps; models ("I do"), practises together ("we do"), then students practise ("you do") [S1]; uses examples from children's lives; lesson builds to a clear end. |
| B2 Checks for understanding | Asks questions to many students (not only the front row or volunteers), uses quick whole-class checks (show fingers, slates, thumbs), waits a few seconds after asking, adjusts teaching when many students are wrong [S3, S4]. |
| B3 Feedback | Tells students specifically what is right and what to do next; corrects misconceptions rather than just saying "wrong"; lets students try again [S5]. |
| B4 Critical thinking | Asks "why" and "how do you know"; asks students to explain, compare, predict, apply to a new example; accepts more than one method [S9]. |

### Area C. Socio-emotional skills
| Element | What you would see or hear at a high level |
|---|---|
| C1 Autonomy | Students make choices, answer in full sentences, ask their own questions. |
| C2 Perseverance | Teacher encourages effort on hard tasks and treats mistakes as useful. |
| C3 Social and collaborative skills | Students work in pairs or small groups with a clear task; listen to and build on each other's answers. |

## 3. The research behind it: core principles (with source codes)

### 3.1 Explicit, step-by-step instruction [S1, S6]
- Start with a short review of earlier learning (daily review).
- Present new material in small steps, with practice after each step; working memory is small, so long explanations overload students [S6].
- Give worked examples and model your thinking aloud.
- Guide students' first practice; check for errors before independent work.
- Aim for a high success rate during practice (most answers right) before moving on.
- Give scaffolds (sentence starters, number lines, picture cues) and remove them gradually.
- Revisit material in weekly and monthly reviews.

### 3.2 Formative assessment: find out what students know during the lesson [S3, S4]
Five key strategies (Wiliam and Black):
1. Make the learning goal and what "success" looks like clear.
2. Plan questions and activities that show what every student understands, not just the confident ones.
3. Give feedback that moves learning forward.
4. Help students check their own work.
5. Use students as resources for each other (peer explanation, pair checking).

In practice:
- **No hands up, cold-calling** (choose who answers) plus **wait time** of 3-5 seconds.
- **All-student responses**: slates or notebooks held up, fingers for multiple-choice (1/2/3/4), thumbs up or down.
- **Hinge question**: one quick multiple-choice question at the key point of the lesson, whose wrong options reveal common misconceptions; the answers decide whether to move on or re-teach.
- **Exit check**: 2-3 quick questions at the end, so the next lesson can start from where students really are.

### 3.3 Feedback [S5]
- Among the highest-impact, lowest-cost practices (EEF Toolkit: about +6 months' progress, strong evidence).
- Good feedback is specific, about the task (not the person), timely, and says what to do next.
- Oral feedback during the lesson counts; written comments are not required.
- Praise effort and strategy ("you checked by adding back, well done"), not just "good".

### 3.4 Make learning stick: retrieval and spaced practice [S7]
- **Practice testing** (low-stakes quizzes, recalling without looking) and **spaced practice** (returning to a topic over days and weeks) have the strongest evidence of the common learning techniques.
- Rereading and copying from the board have weak evidence: replace some copying with recall.
- Mixing problem types (interleaving) helps students learn to choose the right method.

### 3.5 Metacognition and self-regulation [S5]
- Teach students to plan, monitor and check their work ("What will I do first? How do I know my answer is right?"). EEF: about +7-8 months, low cost.
- The teacher models thinking aloud; students explain their reasoning.

### 3.6 Teach at the right level [S10, S11]
- Many children in Classes 3-8 cannot yet read a simple paragraph or do basic arithmetic (see ASER [S15]). Teaching only from the grade textbook leaves them behind.
- **Teaching at the Right Level (TaRL)**, developed by Pratham and tested in several randomised evaluations in India, groups children by current level for part of the day and builds foundational reading and maths with simple activities. It produced some of the largest learning gains measured in education research [S10].
- **Structured pedagogy** (clear daily lesson guides, matching student materials, teacher coaching) and **targeted instruction** are rated "great buys" by the Global Education Evidence Advisory Panel [S11].
- In a lesson, look for: does the teacher know which students are behind, and give them a task they can actually do?

### 3.7 Foundational literacy and numeracy (Classes 1-3) [S8, S9]
NIPUN Bharat goal: by the end of Class 3 every child reads with understanding and does basic maths.
- **Oral language first**: talk, stories, rhymes, conversation in the child's home language.
- **Decoding**: explicit teaching of letter-sound (akshara-sound) links, blending, regular reading practice with feedback.
- **Reading with comprehension**: ask what happened, why, what next; not just reading aloud in chorus.
- **Writing**: from aksharas and words to writing their own sentences.
- **Numeracy**: number sense with concrete objects (sticks, stones, bundles of ten) before pictures and then symbols; place value; mental maths; shapes and measurement through activities; word problems from daily life.
- Chorus repetition alone is a warning sign: the teacher cannot tell which children can actually read.

### 3.8 Language of instruction and multilingual classrooms [S9]
- NEP 2020 and NCF-SE 2023: teach in the child's home or mother tongue wherever possible, at least up to Class 5, and use the home language as a bridge to the second and third languages.
- In English- or semi-English-medium classes, check whether children understand the words: allow explanations in the home language, then build the English vocabulary.

### 3.9 Active, joyful, inquiry-based learning [S9]
- NCF-SE 2023 asks for conceptual understanding over rote recall: discussion, activities, experiments, projects, play-based learning in the early years, and connecting school learning to the child's surroundings.
- Science: let students observe, predict, test and explain (even with simple materials); a demonstration by the teacher alone is weaker than students doing it.
- Activity is only useful if it is linked to a clear learning goal and followed by a check of what was learnt.

### 3.10 Multi-grade classrooms
- Plan one shared theme with tasks at different levels; teach one group directly while others do independent or peer work; use older or stronger students as peer tutors (EEF: peer tutoring about +5-6 months [S5]); rotate the teacher's direct time fairly between groups.

### 3.11 Inclusion
- Call on girls and boys equally; watch who is never asked.
- Seat children with visual, hearing or attention needs near the teacher; give extra time; use more than one way to show the idea (objects, pictures, speech).
- Notice children who are silent through the whole lesson.

## 4. Common patterns to look for (and what to suggest)

| What you might see | Why it matters | What to suggest (pick 1-2) |
|---|---|---|
| Teacher talks for most of the lesson; students only copy or repeat | Little thinking or practice by students; no evidence of who understood | Break into "explain 5 minutes, students try 5 minutes"; add one all-student check [S1, S3] |
| Questions only answered by volunteers or the front row | The teacher hears from the strongest students and assumes everyone understood | Cold-call with names; slates or fingers for everyone; wait 3-5 s [S3] |
| "Did you understand? Yes ma'am" | Not a real check | Ask a question that requires using the idea; hinge question [S4] |
| Chorus reading or chorus answers | Hides children who cannot read or answer | Individual or pair reading with a short comprehension question [S8] |
| Long copying from the blackboard | Low-value use of time | Shorten copying; replace part of it with recall or practice [S7] |
| Mistakes answered with "wrong, sit down" | Students learn to stay quiet | Ask how they got it; correct the misconception; let them try again [S5] |
| Lesson pitched at the textbook while many students cannot read or calculate | Students cannot follow | Quick level check; group by level for part of the period [S10] |
| Same issue noted at earlier visits and still present | The earlier action plan did not work | Make the next step smaller and concrete; demonstrate it; agree a date to check [S12] |
| Harsh language, threats, or physical punishment | Harmful and illegal (RTE Act) | Flag as top priority for the CRP to address directly |

## 5. Reading student answers (step 3 of a visit)

For each answer, decide:
- **Correct and explained**: understood and can explain why. Strong evidence the lesson worked.
- **Correct, no reasoning**: may be memorised. Ask "how do you know?" next time.
- **Partly correct**: has part of the idea; note what is missing.
- **Misconception**: a consistent wrong idea (for example "a bigger number of digits after the decimal point means a bigger number"). Name the misconception: it is the most useful thing for the teacher.
- **No answer or off-topic**: could be language, confidence, or not understanding. Do not over-interpret one silent child.

Then look across all the students: if most share the same gap, it is a teaching point for the lesson, not an individual problem. Weigh student answers more heavily than the teacher's own talk: learning is what students show, not what was said.

## 6. Writing the 4-5 questions the CRP asks students (step 3)

- Base them on **today's lesson** (topic, transcript, textbook chapter) and the class level.
- Use the **language of instruction**, short and spoken-friendly; no trick questions; never personal or sensitive questions.
- Mix levels:
  1. **Recall**: a fact or word from today's lesson.
  2. **Understand**: explain in your own words or give an example.
  3. **Apply**: use it on a new, simple example from daily life.
  4. **Reason**: why or how do you know; spot the mistake in a worked example.
  5. (Optional) **Foundational check**: for Classes 1-5, read one sentence aloud and say what it means, or a simple sum, to see whether a learning-level gap is behind the difficulty.
- For each question give: the question, what a good answer contains, and the common wrong answer or misconception to listen for.
- Advise the CRP to ask **different students** (a front, middle and back row student; girls and boys), not only volunteers, and to record answers without names ("Student A").

## 7. Writing the result for the CRP (step 4)

Structure:
1. **Overall picture** in 2-3 plain sentences.
2. **What went well**: 2-3 strengths, each with evidence.
3. **Main areas to grow**: 1-2 priorities, each with evidence, why it matters (with a source code), and a concrete practice to try.
4. **What students showed**: summary of the student answers and any shared misconception.
5. **Compared with earlier visits**: improved, same, or worse, for each earlier issue.
6. **Agreed next steps**: 1-3 actions with who does it and a realistic date (usually 2-4 weeks), plus what the CRP will look for next time.
7. **Confidence**: high, medium or low, and why (recording quality, length, missing student answers...).

Tone: respectful, encouraging, specific. Suggest; do not command. Write so it can be read out to the teacher.

## 8. Coaching the teacher (how the CRP should use this) [S12, S13]

- Coaching that is **individual, regular, focused on specific practices, and followed up** improves teaching (Kraft, Blazar and Hogan meta-analysis of 60 causal studies [S12]). Larger programmes worked less well than small focused ones, so keep the focus narrow.
- Start with a strength; agree **one** change; **show** how (model a 5-minute bit of the lesson); let the teacher try; agree when you will check.
- Ask the teacher what they noticed first ("Which students do you think understood? How do you know?") before giving your view.
- Follow up on the same practice at the next visit before adding a new one.
